\begin{abstract}
Log-Structured Merge (LSM) trees optimize write throughput by buffering mutations in memory and asynchronously flushing Sorted String Tables (SSTables) to persistent storage. However, synchronizing concurrent point lookups against dynamic background file additions, deletions, and flushes presents a fundamental concurrency challenge. In coarse-grained locking architectures (Approach~A), holding a manager-wide reader-writer lock (\texttt{sync.RWMutex.RLock}) across physical storage reads creates severe lock contention, stalling incoming write transactions and background flush publications. Industrial engines often mitigate this bottleneck using immutable reader snapshots (Approach~B, e.g., RocksDB's \texttt{SuperVersion}), but this introduces allocation and atomic reference-counting churn. This paper investigates an essential architectural question: \emph{How much of the concurrency benefit attributed to immutable reader snapshots can be obtained by shortening the manager-lock critical section using simpler reader-lifetime management (Approach~A-min)?}

We formalize, implement, and evaluate three synchronization strategies within a dependency-free Go LSM engine across two distinct experimental phases: Phase~I (960 prospective trials on Linux ext4 in WSL2) comparing Approach~A against Approach~B, and Phase~II (a 60-trial audited campaign across 18 complete matched triplets on Windows 11 NTFS) comparing Approach~A, Approach~A-min, and Approach~B. Our empirical findings demonstrate that shortening the manager lock critical section to memory-only metadata retrieval substantially mitigates writer starvation, increasing completed writes by 3.3$\times$ to 18.5$\times$ in Phase~I and up to 4.6$\times$ in Phase~II. Crucially, across all 18 complete matched triplets in Phase~II, Approach~A-min demonstrates practical equivalence with Approach~B within a $\pm 5\%$ equivalence band (mean relative difference $-1.65\%$, 95\% bootstrap CI $[-4.94\%, +1.43\%]$, median ratio $1.0000$). Furthermore, under high reader concurrency ($r=16$) and high arrival load, Approach~A suffered systematic publication queue collapse, failing preregistered 10-second deadlines in four independent trials due to 300--640~ms writer lock-drain delays. However, we also identify a critical operational boundary: under rapid reader-set churn ($>4$ flushes/second), snapshot management in Approach~B imposes a 26\% to 37\% throughput penalty at low concurrency ($r \le 4$). We conclude that lightweight reader descriptor leasing captures the core concurrency advantages of immutable snapshotting while avoiding descriptor allocation churn.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Log-Structured Merge (LSM) trees~\cite{oneil1996lsm,dean2008leveldb,rocksdb} form the core storage architecture of modern high-throughput distributed databases. By absorbing incoming mutations into volatile in-memory buffers (Memtables) backed by a Write-Ahead Log (WAL), LSM trees convert random writes into sequential disk operations. As Memtables reach capacity thresholds, background workers freeze and flush them to persistent storage as immutable Sorted String Tables (SSTables), while concurrent merge-sorting workers (compactions) reconcile overlapping key ranges to bound read amplification.

While the physical layout and compaction policies of LSM trees have been extensively studied~\cite{lu2016wisckey,dayan2017monkey}, the synchronization mechanisms governing concurrent queries over dynamically evolving SSTable sets introduce critical performance tradeoffs. In a baseline multi-threaded storage engine, a point query traverses the active Memtable, immutable frozen Memtables, and an ordered collection of on-disk SSTables. To ensure that background flushes and compactions can safely unlink obsolete SSTables without triggering use-after-free panics or data races, naive implementations coordinate query execution using a manager-wide reader-writer lock (\texttt{sync.RWMutex})~\cite{bernstein1983concurrency}.

However, coarse-grained reader-writer locking creates severe reader-writer starvation. When reader threads hold shared read locks (\texttt{RLock}) while performing blocking I/O against SSTables on disk, incoming write transactions and background flush publications attempting to acquire the exclusive lock (\texttt{Lock}) are blocked. In Go's write-preferring \texttt{sync.RWMutex}, queued writers block subsequent readers, triggering catastrophic throughput collapse, tail latency inflation, and publication queue convoying.

To mitigate mutex contention, production engines employ reader versioning. LevelDB~\cite{dean2008leveldb} coordinates SSTables via reference-counted \texttt{Version} structures, releasing the main engine mutex during block index lookups. RocksDB~\cite{rocksdb} unifies this pattern through \texttt{SuperVersion}, consolidating mutable Memtables, immutable Memtables, and active SSTables into a single atomic descriptor that queries pin with atomic reference counting. While effective, immutable snapshot pinning introduces overheads of its own: every flush or compaction must allocate a new descriptor, atomically swap pointers, and manage deferred epoch reclamation.

This paper addresses the following architectural question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{How much of the concurrency benefit attributed to immutable reader snapshots can be obtained by shortening the manager-lock critical section using simpler reader-lifetime management?}
\end{quote}

Rather than claiming reader reference-counting or snapshot pinning as fundamentally novel inventions, our contribution is an independent, controlled, dependency-free architectural dissection implemented in Go. We formalize three synchronization models over identical underlying storage geometry:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Approach A (Coarse RWMutex)}: A single manager-wide lock spanning memory search and physical disk I/O.
    \item \textbf{Approach A-min (Descriptor Leasing)}: A shortened manager-lock critical section that leases individual SSTable reader descriptors with atomic reference tracking across active and retired sets, coupled with a fail-closed shutdown barrier.
    \item \textbf{Approach B (Immutable Snapshots)}: A \texttt{SuperVersion}-style atomic snapshot pointer swap where point queries pin the entire reader set and execute disk lookups without holding manager locks.
\end{enumerate}

Through two distinct experimental campaigns—Phase~I (960 independent trials on WSL2 Linux ext4) and Phase~II (a 60-trial audited campaign across 18 complete matched triplets on Windows 11 NTFS)—we evaluate write starvation thresholds, publication queue mechanics, open-loop arrival latencies, and lifecycle management overheads.

\section{System Architecture and Data Structures}

\subsection{Memory Tier and Multi-Version Tombstones}
The storage engine maintains an active in-memory Memtable backed by a probabilistic SkipList~\cite{pugh1990skiplists} with geometric node height distribution ($p = 0.5$) capped at 32 levels. Writes append sequentially to an on-disk Write-Ahead Log (WAL). Each transaction commits with a monotonically increasing 64-bit sequence number $N_{seq}$ bitwise packed with a tombstone flag:
\begin{equation}
S_{packed} = (N_{seq} \ll 8) \mid F_{tombstone}
\label{eq:packed}
\end{equation}
Bitwise inversion ensures that newer versions sort before older versions during SkipList traversal.

\subsection{Disk Tier Geometry and Compaction}
When the active Memtable exceeds a threshold (e.g., 64MB, or 1MB/16KB in controlled benchmarks), it is appended to an immutable \texttt{Frozenmem} slice and scheduled for flushing. The flusher constructs an SSTable formatted with 4KB data blocks and a sparse two-level block index at the file footer. Background compaction merges overlapping SSTables via a multi-way merge sort, streaming deduplicated keys into consolidated files.

\section{LSM Synchronization Strategies}

\subsection{Approach A: Coarse-Grained RWMutex Coordination}
In Approach A, thread safety across tiered lookups is enforced by a single manager-wide \texttt{sync.RWMutex} (\texttt{m.Mu}):

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Go, caption={Approach A point lookup and flush publication}]
// Point Query (Approach A)
func (m *Manager) Get(key string, readTs uint64) (string, error) {
    m.Mu.RLock()
    defer m.Mu.RUnlock()
    return m.lookupTiered(key, readTs)
}

// SSTable Publication (Approach A)
func (m *Manager) publishFlush(mem *Memtable, reader *SSTableReader) {
    m.Mu.Lock()
    defer m.Mu.Unlock()
    m.ActiveSSreaders = append(m.ActiveSSreaders, reader)
    m.removeFrozenMemtableLocked(mem)
}
\end{lstlisting}

Because \texttt{m.Mu.RLock()} is held during disk I/O, concurrent writers calling \texttt{m.Mu.Lock()} stall until all active readers complete. In Go, queued write locks block subsequent read locks, creating long queue delays.

\subsection{Approach A-min: Minimal Descriptor Leasing}
Approach A-min shortens the critical section: the manager lock is held \emph{only} while reading volatile memory buffers and leasing active SSTable descriptors. Once leased, readers execute disk I/O completely outside \texttt{m.Mu}:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Go, caption={Approach A-min reader leasing and publication}]
// Point Query (Approach A-min)
func (m *Manager) Get(key string, readTs uint64) (string, error) {
    m.Mu.RLock()
    val, err, found := m.lookupMemoryTiers(key, readTs)
    if found { m.Mu.RUnlock(); return val, err }
    // Lease active descriptors under short RLock
    readers := m.leaseActiveReadersLocked()
    m.Mu.RUnlock()
    defer m.releaseReaderLeases(readers)

    // Disk I/O executed with ZERO manager locks held
    return m.searchLeasedReaders(readers, key, readTs)
}
\end{lstlisting}

To prevent use-after-free when background compactions delete SSTables, Approach A-min tracks reader leases on \emph{both} active and retired descriptors. At engine shutdown, \texttt{Close()} executes a fail-closed barrier: if an outstanding lease remains past a timeout, the engine refuses to unlink descriptors and returns an explicit error identifying the unreleased leases.

\subsection{Approach B: Immutable Pinned Reader Snapshots}
Approach B implements atomic snapshot pinning inspired by RocksDB's \texttt{SuperVersion}:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Go, caption={Approach B reader snapshot pinning}]
type ReaderSnapshot struct {
    id         uint64
    readers    []*SharedReader
    activePins atomic.Int64
    state      atomic.Int32 // Published, Retired, Reclaimed
}

func (m *Manager) Get(key string, readTs uint64) (string, error) {
    snap := m.acquireCurrentSnapshot() // O(1) atomic pin
    defer m.releaseSnapshot(snap)
    return snap.lookupSSTables(key, readTs)
}
\end{lstlisting}

During publication, the background worker constructs a new snapshot descriptor, atomically swaps the pointer via \texttt{m.currentSnapshot}, and marks the previous snapshot as \texttt{Retired}. When a retired snapshot's pin counter drains to zero, an asynchronous worker closes underlying file descriptors and deletes obsolete files.

\section{Experimental Methodology}

\subsection{Phase I: Historical WSL2 Linux Campaign}
Phase~I evaluated Approach~A vs. Approach~B on native Linux ext4 within WSL2 (Ubuntu 24.04, kernel 6.6.87.2-WSL2) on an AMD Ryzen 7 7445HS (6P/12L, 15.2~GiB RAM). Durability was enforced via synchronous WAL fsync (\texttt{wal\_sync=true}). The campaign comprised 960 trials ($N = 10$ per cell across 6 workloads and 8 reader concurrencies $r \in [1, 32]$).

\subsection{Phase II: Windows 11 Native Campaign (Phase I7)}
Phase~II evaluated Approach~A, Approach~A-min, and Approach~B on native Windows 11 NTFS (Samsung NVMe SSD). Timing was measured via Windows \texttt{QueryPerformanceCounter} ($100\text{ ns}$ resolution). Requests followed a deterministic, non-overlapping stream derived from SHA-256 counter PRNGs. Write workloads were paced with high-precision sleep loops to evaluate matched offered write rates.

Phase~II was planned for 330 confirmatory trials across 366 runlist rows. In accordance with prospective protocol stopping rules ($N_{\text{fail}} = 10$), the campaign halted after 60 trial attempts. All 60 trials were preserved, producing 18 complete experimental triplets across Blocks E1, E2, E3, E4, and E5.

\section{Phase I Empirical Evaluation (WSL2 Linux)}

Table~\ref{tab:phase1_results} summarizes representative cells from the 960-trial Phase~I campaign.

\subsection{Workload B1: Writer Starvation Mitigation}
In Approach A, readers holding \texttt{m.Mu.RLock()} during disk reads stall writers attempting to commit WAL records. As reader concurrency increases from $r=1$ to $r=32$, completed writes in Approach A collapse from 270 down to 60. By releasing manager locks prior to physical block reads, Approach B substantially mitigates write starvation, increasing completed writes by **3.3$\times$ to 18.5$\times$** (completing 2,573 writes at $r=8$ vs. 139 in A). Concurrently, read throughput increases by up to **2.07$\times$** (260.3 vs. 125.5 ops/s at $r=4$).

\subsection{Workload B2: Tail Latency Dynamics}
Under periodic 500ms flushes, whole-trial throughput differences remain statistically inconclusive (95\% bootstrap CIs overlap 1.0). However, Approach B exhibits substantial suppression of read tail latency at low concurrency ($r=2, 4$), dropping 95th-percentile read latency by **73\% to 76\%** (48.9 ms vs. 181.0 ms at $r=4$). Confirmatory sham-flush tests revealed that this tail reduction occurs even without physical file changes, indicating that it is driven primarily by avoiding exclusive write-lock queue convoying in Go's \texttt{sync.RWMutex}.

\subsection{Workload D: Rapid Churn Negative Tradeoff}
When Memtable thresholds are reduced to 16KB (triggering $>4$ flushes/s), Approach B incurs a **26\% to 37\% throughput penalty** at low concurrency ($r=1$: 0.63$\times$ [0.55, 0.70]; $r=4$: 0.74$\times$ [0.65, 0.90]). The computational overhead of allocating snapshot descriptors, managing atomic reference counts, and executing deferred retirement outweighs concurrency gains when reader threads are few. Only when concurrency reaches $r \ge 16$ do parallel query gains amortize descriptor lifecycle management overheads.

\subsection{Approach A Treatment Deadline Failures (E2 and E4)}
Four triplets exhibited failure in Approach A due to missed publication deadlines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Block E2 (\texttt{pub\_physical})}: Exactly 20 physical flushes were scheduled at 500ms intervals. In Approaches A-min and B, 20/20 flushes completed within the 10.0s window in every trial. In Approach A, flushes stalled waiting for 16 concurrent readers to release \texttt{RLock}, holding write locks for 300--640~ms per flush. Cumulative delays pushed Event 19 to finish at $t = 10.26\text{ s}$ ($260\text{ ms}$ late), resulting in 19/20 in-window flushes in Triplet 01 and 17/20 in Triplet 09.
    \item \textbf{Block E4 ($L_3$ Arrival Rate)}: Under $14.58\text{ ops/s}$ offered arrivals, Approach A suffered severe queue buildup, realizing only 10/20 in-window flushes (10 late) in Triplet 02 and 15/20 (5 late) in Triplet 06, forcing 3--4 requests to be abandoned. Approaches A-min and B completed 20/20 flushes in-window with zero abandoned requests.
\end{itemize}
These failures represent physical manifestations of writer queue convoying under coarse locking, directly validating the research hypothesis.

\section{Methodological Disclosures and Audits}

\subsection{Phase I Overlap Semantics Audit}
Instrumentation in Phase~I revealed positive \texttt{reads\_during\_publication} counts in Approach A despite holding an exclusive lock. Unit tests confirmed this was an artifact of reader drain delay: counters recorded reads that initiated under \texttt{RLock} before publication began and completed while the worker queued for \texttt{Lock}. Exactly zero reads executed while \texttt{Lock} was held.

\subsection{Phase II E5 All-Miss Read Path Mechanism}
In Block E5 ($h=0$, all-miss), read throughput was unexpectedly high ($\sim 3,000\text{ ops/s}$) and $p_{50}$ latency low ($2.34\text{--}2.44\text{ ms}$). Code investigation revealed that absent keys generated by \texttt{keystream.go} ($\text{index} \ge 10,001$) strictly exceeded the populated database range ($0\text{--}9,999$). In \texttt{sstableReader.go}, binary search over the block index resolved immediately to the final block index. Readers inspected only the last 4KB data block, encountered EOF, and returned \texttt{ErrNotFound}. This final block remained pinned in OS page cache across queries, bypassing multi-block disk traversal. Block E5 does not validate an exhaustive multi-level scan.

\subsection{Pebble Comparison Exclusion}
Pebble benchmark runs in Block E7 were invalidated by two issues:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Envelope Incompatibility}: \texttt{pebble\_runner.exe} omitted \texttt{result.timestamps}, causing the orchestrator to flag all runs as \texttt{INVALID}.
    \item \textbf{Buffer Exhaustion}: Fixed 1M-sample buffers per reader filled after ~5 seconds at 1.88M ops/s, dropping over 10.45 million latency samples ($>50\%$ of reads).
\end{enumerate}
Pebble data are excluded from primary comparisons.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Truncated Factorial Execution}: Phase~II halted at 60 of 330 planned trials under the stopping rule ($N_{\text{fail}} = 10$). While 18 complete matched triplets were preserved across 5 blocks, full factorial concurrency scaling ($r=1\dots 32$ across all workloads) was not executed.
    \item \textbf{Unavailable Drift Baseline}: Due to runner omission of \texttt{"D3"} in \texttt{cmd/i7trial/main.go}, initial drift trials failed ($N_{\text{start}} = 0$). Campaign drift ratio cannot be computed, leaving environmental drift over the 26.4-minute run unbounded by the primary control.
    \item \textbf{Unrun Windows Race Detector}: Windows binaries were compiled without \texttt{-race} due to cgo toolchain unavailability. Thread safety was verified independently on Linux ext4.
    \item \textbf{Single-Host Storage Environment}: All Phase~II trials operated on a single AMD Ryzen 7 laptop on NTFS with active OS page cache. Findings characterize in-process memory-lock bottlenecks rather than physical disk head serialization.
    \item \textbf{Unpooled Cross-Phase Datasets}: Phase~I (WSL2 Linux) and Phase~II (Windows NTFS) represent distinct experimental platforms. Their effect sizes are not statistically pooled.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This study evaluated reader-writer synchronization architectures in Go LSM trees to determine whether simpler reader-lifetime management can capture the concurrency benefits of immutable reader snapshots. Our empirical findings demonstrate that shortening the manager-lock critical section using lightweight descriptor leasing (Approach~A-min) eliminates Approach~A's publication queue convoying and write starvation, achieving quantitative parity with immutable snapshot pinning (Approach~B) within a $\pm 5\%$ equivalence band across 18 complete matched triplets. Furthermore, descriptor leasing avoids the 26\%--37\% throughput penalty observed in snapshot pinning under rapid reader-set churn. Storage engine architects can achieve the primary concurrency benefits of snapshot isolation through fine-grained descriptor leasing without incurring full snapshot lifecycle overhead.
